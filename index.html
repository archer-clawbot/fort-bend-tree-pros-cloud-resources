Start with safe observations, not a diagnosis

Stay outside the likely drop zone and record only what can be seen from a stable ground-level position. Useful notes include whether the condition appeared suddenly, which part of the tree is involved, what occupied area or structure is nearby, and whether the only driveway or gate is blocked. Photographs are most useful when they show the whole tree, the visible concern, and the property context. A close image without scale or surroundings may omit the information needed to plan access.

Do not climb a damaged tree, stand beneath suspended wood, pull branches with a vehicle, or touch a tree or limb that may contact a utility line. Potential electrical contact belongs with the utility or emergency services first. A public checklist cannot determine tree condition, prescribe pruning, prove the cause of a failure, predict insurance coverage, or replace an on-site assessment.

Build a comparable estimate scope

Before comparing totals, ask each proposed scope to identify the tree or numbered work area, the intended result, equipment and crew access, nearby fences or surfaces, debris handling, cleanup, and what remains excluded. For trimming, describe the clearance or branch concern rather than requesting a vague percentage. For removal, clarify whether stump work is included and how the area should be left. For several trees, divide the list into immediate, planned, and optional items so unlike scopes are not compared as though they were equivalent.

Ground conditions, gate width, pools, roofs, detached structures, neighboring property, utility routes, and the distance between the street and work area can change the plan. Keep proposal versions, written clarifications, photographs, change orders, and completion notes together. A clear scope does not guarantee provider availability, workmanship, price, or outcome; it makes assumptions and responsibilities easier to see.
